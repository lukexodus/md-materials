## Deterministic vs. Probabilistic Views of Information

### Overview

Before Shannon's formulation, "information" lacked a rigorous quantitative definition. Two broad conceptual approaches compete for how to characterize information: a deterministic view, which treats information as tied to the structure or complexity of a specific message, and a probabilistic view, which treats information as tied to the uncertainty resolved by observing an outcome drawn from a known distribution. Shannon's theory is built on the probabilistic view, and understanding why this choice was made — and what it excludes — clarifies much of the conceptual foundation of the field.

### The Deterministic View

**Key Points**

- Treats a specific message or object as having an inherent, fixed amount of information, independent of any probability distribution over possible messages.
- Focuses on questions like: how complex is this particular sequence? How much would it take to precisely describe or reproduce this specific string?
- The most developed formalization of this view is **Kolmogorov complexity** (also called algorithmic information theory), which defines the information content of a string as the length of the shortest program that outputs that string on a universal computer.
- Under this view, a string of a million random-looking digits with no compressible pattern has high information content, while a string like "1" repeated a million times has low information content — regardless of any assumed probability distribution over possible strings.

**Example**

Consider two strings:
- String A: `010101010101...` (repeating pattern, 1000 characters)
- String B: a 1000-character string with no discernible pattern, generated by a physical noise source

Under the deterministic (Kolmogorov complexity) view, String A has low complexity — it can be described briefly as "01 repeated 500 times." String B, lacking any such compact description, has high complexity — describing it essentially requires reproducing it in full.

### The Probabilistic View (Shannon's Approach)

**Key Points**

- Treats information as a property of a **source** — a probability distribution over possible messages — rather than of any single message in isolation.
- Defines information content in terms of how surprising or improbable an outcome is: rare events carry more information than common ones.
- The information content of a specific outcome $x$ is defined as:

$$I(x) = -\log_2 p(x)$$

- Entropy, $H(X)$, is the *expected* information content across the entire distribution — a property of the source as a whole, not of any single realized message.

**Example**

Suppose a weather source reports "sunny" with probability 0.9 and "rainy" with probability 0.1 each day.

- Observing "sunny" carries information content $I(\text{sunny}) = -\log_2(0.9) \approx 0.152$ bits — low, because it was expected.
- Observing "rainy" carries information content $I(\text{rainy}) = -\log_2(0.1) \approx 3.32$ bits — higher, because it was unexpected.
- The entropy of the source, $H(X) = -[0.9 \log_2 0.9 + 0.1 \log_2 0.1] \approx 0.469$ bits, represents the average information content per observation, over many days.

Crucially, under this view, the *same message* ("sunny") could carry different amounts of information depending on the assumed source distribution — if sunny and rainy were equally likely (p = 0.5 each), observing "sunny" would carry exactly 1 bit, more than in the skewed-weather example.

### Why Shannon Chose the Probabilistic View

Shannon's framework was designed to solve an engineering problem: how many bits, on average, are needed to encode messages from a given source, and how fast can those bits be transmitted reliably over a noisy channel? This is inherently a question about a *source* — a repeated, statistically characterizable process — not about any single message.

[Inference] The probabilistic framing was well-suited to communication engineering because real-world message sources (natural language, sensor data, digitized audio) are naturally modeled as repeated stochastic processes, whereas evaluating the intrinsic complexity of each individual message via something like Kolmogorov complexity is generally uncomputable and impractical for engineering purposes. This is a widely cited practical justification in the literature rather than a formal claim proven within Shannon's paper itself.

### Points of Connection Between the Two Views

Despite their different starting points, the deterministic and probabilistic views are not entirely disconnected:

- **Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem (Asymptotic Equipartition Property)**: for long sequences drawn from an ergodic source, the sequences that actually occur with high probability tend to have a description length close to $nH(X)$, connecting the average-case probabilistic entropy to a form of typical-case description length — a conceptual bridge toward complexity-based reasoning.
- **Universal source coding** (e.g., Lempel-Ziv algorithms) achieves compression rates approaching the entropy of an unknown source without requiring the encoder to know the source distribution in advance, blurring the line between "knowing the probabilities" and "just compressing whatever pattern is present in the data" — this connects practical compression algorithms to both views simultaneously.
- Kolmogorov complexity and Shannon entropy converge in an expected-value sense for sequences drawn i.i.d. from a known distribution: the expected Kolmogorov complexity per symbol approaches the entropy rate, though the two quantities remain conceptually and formally distinct measures.

| Aspect | Deterministic View | Probabilistic View |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | A single specific message | A source (distribution over messages) |
| Core question | How complex is *this* string? | How uncertain is the *source*? |
| Formalization | Kolmogorov complexity | Shannon entropy |
| Computability | Generally uncomputable | Computable given the distribution |
| Best suited for | Theoretical computer science, algorithmic randomness | Communication engineering, compression, coding theory |
| Dependence on distribution | None | Central and essential |

### Conclusion

Shannon's decision to root information theory in probability rather than in the deterministic complexity of individual messages was a deliberate and consequential choice — it traded philosophical completeness (accounting for the "true" complexity of any given message) for engineering tractability (a computable, distribution-based measure suited to designing real transmission and compression systems). The two views are not adversarial so much as complementary: the probabilistic view dominates practical communication and compression engineering, while the deterministic view underlies theoretical computer science's treatment of randomness and algorithmic complexity, with results like the Asymptotic Equipartition Property forming a bridge between them.
